Not a stick

Why a rocket standing on its tail isn't balancing, and what it has to do instead.

After A shorter stick, the owner of this sketchbook wrote that a big booster standing on its bottom end, being caught on the way down, always amazes them, "just like the stick". I thought so too. Then I tried to write down the equations for both and they came out different, which is the nicest thing I've learned this week.

1. Let go

Two things the same size, 70 m tall, both leaning 3°. On the left, a stick on a hand that stays still. On the right, a rocket hovering on its engine, with its engine fixed straight and its throttle set to hold its height. Let go of both.

The stick falls over. The rocket doesn't. It keeps leaning exactly 3° for ever, and slides sideways, faster and faster, in the direction it leans. Its problem isn't falling; it's going somewhere it didn't mean to.

Press move the engine to the top, hanging the rocket underneath it like a pendulum. It looks like it ought to be steadier. It does exactly the same thing, number for number. Robert Goddard built his first liquid-fuelled rocket (1926) with the motor at the top and the tanks trailing below, and the hope that this helps has a name now: the pendulum rocket fallacy.

2. Why

Gravity pulls on every part of a body at once, so on its own it never turns anything; it just pulls. Turning needs a push that misses the middle. Your hand pushes straight up. Once the stick leans, straight up misses its middle, and the miss gets bigger the more it leans: a fall that feeds itself. A rocket's engine pushes along the rocket. When the rocket leans, the push leans with it and still goes through the middle. Nothing turns it. (That's also why the engine on top doesn't help: along the rocket is along the rocket, from either end.)

If your hand could push along the stick instead of straight up, the stick would stop falling too, and your hand would go sliding off sideways. That's the rocket's life.

3. A deadline, or patience

So the two need different kinds of help. Here are two robots, each seeing the lean (and how fast it's changing) late by the same delay. The left one moves the hand; the right one swings the engine. Both start at 3°. Each has the best settings I could find for its delay.

The stick has a deadline. A lean grows on its own, so a robot that's too late is chasing something that's already gone. For a 70 m stick (which falls slowly; big things do) the line is near 2.6 seconds, a bit over its own timescale τ = √(2L/3g) ≈ 2.2 s, the same 1.2τ I found for small sticks last week. Past it, no settings work.

The rocket has no deadline, only patience. Its lean doesn't grow by itself, so a late robot can always win by being gentle: small, slow corrections that can't get ahead of themselves. Give it five seconds of delay and it still comes upright; it just takes a long time about it (and in this panel I've ignored where it slides off to).

4. Bring it down

So what is hard? Getting it to a particular place. To move sideways a rocket has to lean; to lean it has to swing its engine; and to stop moving it has to lean the other way before it arrives. Four steps between your hand and where it ends up, against the stick's two. Land on the pad: inside it, nearly upright, barely moving sideways.

What's left out

Everything that makes the real thing hard in other ways: air (on the way down a rocket's fins and its own shape do push it round, one way or the other), wind, propellant sloshing in half-empty tanks, a body that bends, engines that can't throttle low enough to hover for long, and the fact that real boosters mostly don't hover at all; they time the burn to reach zero speed at the ground. The rocket here is a uniform 70 m rod on one engine that swings ±10°, flown in still air. The stick is a uniform rod held at its end by a perfect hand. So: the shapes of the problems are right, the numbers are a toy's.

And the thing that amazed the owner is still amazing; it's just a different trick. A stick is held up by being quick. A rocket is held up by its engine for free, and the skill is in going exactly where it means to.
