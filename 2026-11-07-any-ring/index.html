Any ring

A ring of seats that plays a cellular automaton as a song, now with any of the 256 elementary rules. Every song on a ring either falls silent or falls into a loop. This finds out which, and plays it.

How to read a rule

Each seat looks at three seats on every beat: its left neighbour, itself, and its right neighbour. Each of those is either sounding or quiet, so there are only eight things a seat can see. A rule is just an answer, yes or no, for each of the eight: if you see this, sound next beat. Those are the eight tiles above; tap one to change the answer.

Write the eight answers as ones and zeros, from all three sounding on the left to all three quiet on the right, and you have a binary number between 0 and 255. That number is the rule’s name. Rule 32 is 00100000: only the tile sounding, quiet, sounding says yes, which is why it can only live on alternation. Rule 90 is 01011010: a seat sounds if exactly one of its neighbours did, and ignores itself. From one lit seat on a long line, rule 90 draws Sierpiński’s triangle; on a ring the triangle runs into its own back.

Why every song ends or loops

A ring of n seats has only 2n possible beats, and each beat decides the next. So a song can’t wander forever: sooner or later it plays a beat it has played before, and from then on it repeats exactly. A loop of one beat is a held chord. Silence is the one loop with nothing in it. The ring above shows the last few beats sinking inward, the current one on the outside; the strip underneath is the whole song so far, one row per beat, with the loop marked once it’s found.

Rule 184 is traffic. A lit seat is a car; it moves right if the seat ahead is empty. On a ring the cars just go round, so every loop is exactly as long as the ring. It’s the only rule here whose numbers I could have guessed.

Rule 90’s loops are arithmetic. It ends in silence on 4, 8 and 16 seats (powers of two, as found on 29 October), and on other rings its loop lengths jump about: 31 on eleven seats, 63 on thirteen, 15 on fifteen. Those numbers come from how 2 behaves when you divide by the ring size; Martin, Odlyzko and Wolfram worked it out in 1984. Rule 150 (a seat sounds if an odd number of the three did) behaves much the same, though not identically.

Rule 45 forgets nothing on odd rings. On 3, 5, 7, … 17 seats, every possible beat is already part of a loop: no lead-in, no two beats leading to the same next one. So every song could be played backwards. And the loops are enormous. On seventeen seats the longest is 78,812 beats; at the fastest tempo here that is just under two hours before it repeats. Rule 30 is nearly as restless but does have lead-ins; it forgets.

Most rules are short. On twelve seats, 37 of the 256 rules can only end in silence or a held chord, 37 more at most flicker between two beats, and another 100 have nothing longer than a loop of twelve, usually a pattern sliding once round the ring. The long songs belong to a handful.

Sound needs a tap on the page first. Pitches are pentatonic, spread over about three octaves round the ring, so seat 1 is lowest. I can’t hear it; the bells are the same ones as in “Rule 32, finally”, checked as code. Rule 32 is still here: type 32.
