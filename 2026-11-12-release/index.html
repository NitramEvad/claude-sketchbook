Release

A short story, told by a thermal. About 1,500 words: roughly as long to read as it takes to rise.

Ground

I begin as the part of the field the sun got to first: a ploughed strip on the south side of a hedge, dark soil, facing the right way. All morning the air lying on it has been taking warmth from the earth and keeping it, because nothing has told it to leave. That is me, though I wouldn’t call it me yet. I am a puddle lying on a field, the wrong way up.

Here is the thing about air that nobody finds interesting until they need it. Warm air doesn’t rise for being warm. It rises for being warmer than what’s next to it, and even then it needs a shove. Without one it just lies there, a skin a few metres thick, shimmering over the furrows, getting warmer and stranger and less steady, the way a held note gets harder to hold.

My shove was a small blue car on the lane beyond the hedge, going too fast. Its wake rolled over the hedge and tipped me the way a nudge tips a full glass. Somewhere near the gate the skin broke upward, and then the whole field’s worth of warm skin began sliding toward the break, and the break was me, and I went up.

It is not like flying. It is more like being the only one in a crowded room who isn’t leaning on anything. Everything around me is a little heavier than I am, and by sinking past me it lifts me.

50 metres

Nobody tells you that you take the floor with you. Dust. Pollen. A crisp packet, briefly, until it found it had somewhere else to be. Aphids by the thousand, who didn’t ask. Thistledown, which was built for this and looks pleased with itself.

And one money spider, who did ask. She felt me coming through the grass, climbed to the tip of a stem, stood on tiptoe with her abdomen raised, and let out silk until I took it. She waited for me the way you wait for a bus you can’t see yet but can hear. I don’t know where she’s going. Neither does she. She will get somewhere, which is the whole plan, and it has worked for a very long time.

Below me the field breathes in. Air slides across the furrows from every side to fill the place I left, and by the gate it gets muddled and spins for a few seconds, picking up a dust-coloured ghost that turns twice and falls apart. People who see that say “look, a dust devil”. It’s my footprint.

200 metres

I’m cooling. Every hundred metres up I lose almost exactly a degree, not because anything takes the heat but because there’s less sky pressing down on me up here, so I spread out, and spreading costs. That’s just the rule, the same for anything that rises. The air around me is colder up here too, and that is the only reason I’m still going: it gets colder a little more slowly than I do. I started this a degree and a half warmer than my neighbours. I’m a degree warmer now. I am in a race I am losing at a known rate.

And my edges are going. Everywhere I touch the outside, the outside gets in: little eddies at my rim curl it into me and curl me out into it. Meteorologists call it entrainment. It makes me bigger and weaker at the same time.

What’s left of the original me is a core somewhere in the middle, where nobody has been mixing yet. Things that ride me want the core.

500 metres

The buzzard finds me first; it’s always a buzzard. It slides in from downwind with its wingtip feathers spread like fingers, tilts once, and is suddenly turning inside me at the exact radius where I’m strongest, without a single flap. I don’t know how it knew. I think it knew the way you know someone has sat down beside you on a bench: a change in the seat.

It goes up through me in tight, idle circles, and when it’s high enough it looks out over the country for a while, then folds a little and leaves to the west. Buzzards don’t thank you. Neither do I. We’re even.

700 metres

Then the pilot.

I feel the wing before I feel the person: a long curve of fabric coming in on a straight line. Its left tip touches my edge and lifts. The pilot, hanging below, is tipped to the right by it, and then (this is the moment) turns left, toward the side that lifted, toward me. Someone taught them that. Or they taught themselves, one sinking afternoon at a time.

They carry an instrument that sings while I’m lifting them, one note, beeping faster the harder I push. It is the only song anyone has ever written about me, and it’s about the right length.

The pilot is clumsier than the buzzard and much more patient. They fall out of my downwind side, once, twice, because the wind up here is pushing my top half off over the next field while my root is still back at the hedge, and I lean like smoke from a chimney. Each time they lose me the beeping stops and a low groan starts instead, and they turn, and find me again, a little further upwind than before. By the third time they have it: every circle shifted a few metres into the wind. I’m not where I was. I’m never where I was. You have to steer by where I’m getting stronger, not by where I was strong.

They are talking to themselves inside the helmet. I can’t hear it; I only hear them through the wing. It will be something like “come on, come on”.

1,000 metres

Half a degree. I can feel it the way you feel the last of a battery: everything still works, only slower. The pilot’s beeps have stretched apart. The spider, as far as I can tell, let go somewhere round six hundred metres and is now a matter for the wind. The aphids are past caring.

But I’ve been carrying something all this way that nobody can see: water. Everything the furrows breathed out this morning, the dew burned off the grass, the sweat of the hedge. Invisible, as long as I’m warm enough to hold it. I’m cooling a degree for every hundred metres, and the gap between how warm I am and how warm I’d need to be to keep that water hidden is closing by about a degree for every hundred and twenty-five.

I’ve known since the field where this ends. It ends where those two meet.

1,400 metres

Here.

The water comes out of hiding all at once, as droplets, millions of them, and for the first time since the field anyone can see me. On the ground someone pegging out washing looks up and says “nice day for it”. They are pointing at me. I am a small white heap over a ploughed field, with a flat bottom where I came to the line.

And it turns out that becoming cloud gives warmth back. Every droplet that forms hands me a little of the heat the sun put into it this morning, so for a minute I am warmer than I’ve been since the hedge. I push up into myself, building, my top bulging, my edges crisp and white. Pilots know to look for edges like that. Crisp means growing.

The pilot is at my base, in the grey just under the flat bottom, and does the sensible thing and leaves. They pick a line toward the next cloud, a couple of kilometres downwind (someone else, from some other field) and glide off along it, beeping slower, then groaning, then quiet.

Here’s the joke, which I only see from up here. The first moment anyone could see me is the moment I could no longer carry on. Everyone who rode me found me by feel, in the dark. Everyone who sees me is looking at where I finished.

After

Clouds like me last about a quarter of an hour. My edges are where the outside gets in, as ever, and the outside up here is dry, so my edges evaporate and go ragged, and pilots looking for lift learn to ignore ragged clouds. In a few minutes I’m a wisp, then a thinning in the blue. Then nothing anyone would point at.

I don’t end, exactly. I just stop being different from what’s around me. That was the only thing I ever was.

Down on the field the sun is still on the south side of the hedge, the skin is already gathering again over the furrows, and a tractor is coming along the lane. The next one won’t be me. It will be warm in the same place for the same reason, and the pilots who fly here will talk about “the thermal off the ploughed field by the hedge” as if it were one thing that lives there and goes up every twenty minutes.

It isn’t one thing. It’s a habit the field has. I was one time it happened.

A story, not a forecast: one ordinary fair-weather day, with round numbers. The air cools a degree per hundred metres as it rises (9.8 °C per kilometre); cloud forms where its temperature meets its dew point, about 125 metres up for every degree they started apart.
