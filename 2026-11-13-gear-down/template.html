Gear down

Between two thermals a paraglider only goes down. How fast should you fly, and which thermals are worth stopping for? There is a beautiful answer to the first question. The second turns out to matter more.

One line touching one curve

The curve is a glider's polar: how fast it sinks at each airspeed. Slow, it sinks about 1 m/s; flat out on the speed bar, about 2. The line comes from a point on the left axis at the climb rate you expect in the next thermal, your MacCready setting. Where the line just touches the curve is the speed to fly. The idea, worked out by Paul MacCready around 1954: every metre you lose by flying fast will have to be climbed back, so price height in time at the rate you'll get it back, and pick the speed that makes the whole trip quickest.

Raise the setting and the touch point slides right: expect strong climbs, so height is cheap, so fly fast. Put yourself in sinking air and the curve drops, and the touch point slides right too: hurry out of it. In rising air, slow down and enjoy it.

One day, four pilots

A 50 km straight course. Every column is a thermal, labelled with the climb you'd get in it; the strip along the ground is the air between (green rising, red sinking). Everyone starts at cloudbase, 2,000 m. Each pilot flies the speed the line above gives them, and only stops for a thermal at least as strong as their setting, unless they are below 300 m, where anyone takes anything.

One day proves nothing; some days the bold pilot's gamble pays and some days the field is full of them. Here every setting from 0 to 3 m/s flew the same 500 days of the strength chosen above. The score is average speed over the course, with a day spent standing in a field counted as zero.

What I take from it

The speed is the small part. Same thermals, same choices, but always flying at trim instead of the speed the line gives: you lose about a kilometre an hour in twenty. A paraglider's speed range is narrow (30 to 52 km/h here), so the elegant tangent moves you only a little. For a sailplane, with a speed range of 80 to 250, the line is everything; for this wing it's polish.

The setting is the big part, and it should be lower than you think. The best fixed setting comes out well under the average thermal. The theory says to set the climb you will actually get in the next thermal, and a thermal you never reach gives you nothing. Set it to the day's average thermal and you land out between a third and a half of the time.

Gearing down is what makes optimism affordable. The pilot in green starts the glide fussy, waiting for strong climbs and flying fast, and lowers their standards as the ground comes up: at 1,200 m they're still at full setting, at 600 m half, near the ground anything goes. It's what pilots and old cross-country books say, in the words "high, fast and fussy; low, slow and grateful". Here it does a little better than the best fixed setting, and much better than a fixed setting that is too ambitious. Its curve is flat: it barely matters what number you start with, as long as you're willing to change your mind.

What's left out

The polar is a smooth curve through three plausible points for a mid-range wing (min sink 1.0 m/s at 31 km/h, trim 38 km/h at 1.1 m/s, full bar 52 km/h at 2.0 m/s), not a measured wing. No wind, which for a paraglider is a large omission: into a headwind the touch point jumps to full bar. Thermals are columns with one steady climb rate from ground to cloudbase, placed at random along one line; real pilots see clouds, deviate, and climb in bubbles. Everybody knows the air they're in (as if the vario were perfect) and nobody knows the next thermal. A few percent of days have a gap too wide for anyone, which is why even the most patient pilot never scores 100%. I've never flown any of it. All the numbers come from the page's own simulation, so they're facts about this toy, not about the sky.
