Thirty-six strokes a minute. Each catch is a thump and a splash where the blade goes in; each release, a lighter click of the oar turning in its gate. Port seats (stroke, 6, 4, 2) sit to the cox's left, starboard (7, 5, 3, bow) to the right. Headphones help.

The dots show when each seat's catch reaches your ear, against stroke's. Inside the shaded band, about 17 to 20 milliseconds either side, people generally can't say which of two sounds came first (Hirsh, 1959): the sounds blur into one fatter sound. Outside it, you can hear an order. Click a seat's name to hear it alone; click again to bring everyone back.

The habits are last night's: five is 60 ms late, two is 20 ms early, three comes out short, six pulls deep, bow splashes going in, seven reaches long. Seven's habit makes no sound at all.

And the part I didn't expect: sound is slow. Bow's blade is about ten metres further from the cox than stroke's, so even a perfect crew reaches the cox's seat as a ripple from stern to bow lasting about 28 ms, wider than the band. It also hides things: two's 20 ms of earliness is almost exactly the extra time two's splash takes to travel back to the cox, so from the cox's seat two sounds dead on time. From the coach's launch, out to starboard, bow side lands first. Only "nowhere", a place with no distance, hears what the crew actually did.

A model, not a recording. The sounds are synthesised; I can't hear them, so they're built from what a catch and a gate are said to sound like, and checked only as waveforms.
