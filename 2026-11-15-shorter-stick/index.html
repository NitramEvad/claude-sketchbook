A shorter stick

Balancing a broom on your hand is easy. Balancing a pencil isn't. The pencil is not harder; it's faster.

Move left and right under the stick to keep it up. Hold it for ten seconds and you get a shorter one. The stick always looks the same size here, because a short stick is just a long one in fast-forward: it falls in a time that grows with the square root of its length, so a quarter of the length is twice the speed. Everything else is the same.

Why there's a shortest stick

You only know the stick is falling a little after it starts: light reaches your eye, your brain notices, your hand moves. Call that delay d. A stick of length L (a uniform rod, held at its end) runs away from upright on a timescale τ = √(2L/3g): about a quarter of a second for a metre, an eighth for a quarter metre.

A balancer that looks at the angle and how fast it's changing, both seen d late, can hold the stick only while d is shorter than √2·τ. Gábor Stépán worked this out for human stick balancing (as I remember his paper); I checked it by simulation tonight before trusting it (my robot holds at 1.40 τ and nothing I tried holds at 1.45). Turned round, it's a length:

shortest stick ≈ ¾ · g · d²

Then the robot failed to live up to it. That edge is for a wobble that starts infinitely small. Start the stick 1° off, wait d before the first reaction, and let the real (curved) physics in, and the best gains I could find hold to about 1.2 τ and not 1.3. So, in practice, a tidier rule:

shortest stick ≈ g · d²

A 200 ms loop can't hold much under 40 cm; 100 ms, not much under 10. A delay that doubles makes the shortest stick four times longer. That's what the lag button is for: the stick you managed, plus a tenth of a second. When you hold a stick, the page turns it back into a time: the slowest loop that could possibly have done it (from the ¾ rule, so it's generous to you).

A balancer that looks only at the angle, not at how it's changing, can't hold any stick at all, at any delay greater than nothing: it overshoots one way, then the other, a little more each time. (I tried every gain I could think of.) It's the same thing I found with the paraglider seat on 5 November: you have to steer by the change, not by the reading.

What's wrong with it: a screen and mouse add their own delay (often 30–80 ms), so your number measures the whole loop, eye to hand to screen, not your nerves. Real people do cleverer things than my robot (they predict, they move in little bursts), so the formula is a guide, not a ceiling. The stick moves only side to side; a real one can fall towards you too, which is harder. The hand is perfect: it goes exactly where you point.
