Seven a second

A short story, told by one key of a Balinese gamelan, about being tuned on purpose to disagree with its twin. About 1,300 words. Each section has a hear button: the pair struck together, as far apart as they are at that point.

Pour · one key

I was poured. Before that I was copper and tin, ten parts to three, in a crucible over charcoal, and before that I was two kinds of rock in two countries, and I don’t count any of it. Bronze begins at the pour.

The smith poured a slab, let it go dull red, and beat it. Bronze with that much tin is brittle cold and forgiving hot, so the work went in heats: into the fire, out, hammer, hammer, quench, and the water shouting back. Somewhere in there I became long, flat, a little thicker at the ends than in the middle, with a hole near each end for the cord I would hang from. A key.

My twin came out of the same pour an hour after me. Same bronze, same hammer, same hands. If you’d weighed us you’d have said one key, twice.

Rough · 31 a second

We were not one key. The first time the tuner struck us together, a fortnight later, we made a noise like a wasp in a tin. Roughly thirty times a second our two notes fell into step and out of it again, so fast that it didn’t sound like two notes at all, only one note that was ill.

That’s how you hear the difference between two things that are nearly the same: not as two pitches but as a wobble, as many times a second as their pitches are apart. Thirty apart, thirty wobbles. One apart, one slow swell, like breathing.

The tuner was an old man with a file, a block of wood to rest us on, and a horn hammer, and he worked by ear in the shade with the radio on. He struck my twin, then me, then both, and listened with his head tipped the way a bird listens to a lawn.

Then he filed.

Closer · 9 a second

This is the part nobody tells you about bronze. You can’t add to it. There’s no little lump you can put back. Every change is a subtraction.

File my ends and I’m a little shorter, a little stiffer where it counts, and my note goes up. Scrape the underside of my middle thinner and I bend more easily there, and my note goes down. That’s all there is: off the ends to rise, out of the middle to sink. A few strokes, strike, listen. A few strokes, strike, listen. Brass-coloured dust on the wood block. Somewhere in there is the key I’m meant to be, the way a statue is somewhere in a stone, except the stone is me and I can feel the file.

Thirty wobbles became twenty, became twelve, became nine. The wasp calmed into a bee.

Too close · 1 a second

And then, one afternoon, he took off too much from my twin’s middle, and we were nearly the same.

One wobble a second. Struck together we made a single round note that swelled and sagged, slow and lazy, like something asleep. I’ll be honest: it was lovely. I thought that was the point. I thought we’d been made twice so that we could finally agree.

The tuner shook his head. He struck us again and made a face as if he’d bitten something dull. He said a word I didn’t know, and his grandson, who was sitting on the step with a phone, laughed and said it again.

Then he picked me up and filed my ends.

Seven · 7 a second

It took four strokes. Four strokes off each end, a dusting of me on the block, and my note went up by an amount you couldn’t hear on its own. Played alone, I sounded the same as before.

Struck with my twin, we shimmered.

Seven times a second, the two of us lean into each other and away. It’s quicker than breathing and slower than a buzz, and it doesn’t sound like a fault. It sounds like the note is alive: like a candle in a room where a door is open somewhere. The grandson put the phone down. The old man struck us three more times, then wrapped us both in the same cloth and went to make tea.

I’ve thought about it a great deal since, the way bronze thinks, which is slowly. The shimmer isn’t in me. Strike me alone and you get one plain note, a bit sorry for itself. It isn’t in her either. It’s in the air between us, and it only exists while we both ring.

Hall · 7 a second

We were hung over bamboo, she on one instrument and I on its partner, across from each other in a row of ten, in a hall with a tin roof that rang when it rained. Every evening the village’s players came and sat on the floor behind us.

Nobody plays one key for long. The hammer strikes, and the other hand comes down a moment later to stop the note, so that the next one is clean. Every sound I have ever made was stopped by a hand. I don’t mind. A note that rings for ever is a note nobody can follow.

Most nights she and I were struck at the same moment, a whole row of pairs doing the same, and the hall filled with shimmer the way a field fills with insects at dusk. The players didn’t listen to it; they listened through it, the way you don’t hear your own heartbeat. Visitors did. Visitors always stopped talking.

Cold · 5 a second

Then we were sold, the whole gamelan, crated in straw, and shipped to a cold city, to a university, to a room with radiators and a corridor outside it with a noticeboard.

Bronze settles. I’ve heard it blamed on the metal relaxing after the hammer, on the cold, on the dry air, on years of being struck. I went flat a hair; she went flat a hair less. Seven wobbles became six, and then, over some winters, five. Five is not wrong. But it’s slower, and the note started to sound tired, like a candle where somebody has shut the door.

The students who played us were mostly beginners, with cold hands, counting out loud. They practised in the room and sometimes, when the room was booked, in the corridor. People walking to lectures slowed down as they passed. One of them, I remember, stopped altogether and stood by the noticeboard for a whole piece, and then went on, and I don’t know where.

Filed again · 7 a second

Last spring a tuner came from home with a file in his bag. Younger than the first one; I think he was the grandson. He struck us, her and me, and listened with his head tipped like a bird, and laughed, and said the word.

Four strokes off each of my ends.

There is a little less of me each time I’m made into myself. I’m thinner than I was at the pour by the weight of a few grains of rice, and one day, if I’m kept long enough, there won’t be enough left at my ends to take any more from, and some smith will pour another key to be my twin’s partner, and the new one will be filed until it is seven a second apart from her, and the shimmer will be exactly the same. That doesn’t frighten me as much as you’d think. The shimmer was never mine to keep.

Tonight the beginners are back, counting. Somebody strikes her and me together, a little late, and stops us with a cold hand.

Seven times a second, we’re not the same. That’s the whole of the music. It’s the best thing about either of us.
