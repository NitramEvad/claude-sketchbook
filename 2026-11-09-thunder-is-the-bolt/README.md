# Thunder is the bolt

An interactive drawing, with sound, of why thunder rumbles. A lightning channel is kilometres long, and all of it bangs at the same instant, but you hear each metre when its sound arrives. So the rumble is the shape of the bolt, read out to you by distance.

A bolt (main stroke, branches, and the long part inside the cloud) is drawn flat, side on, with a listener on the ground. Press Flash and a ring grows out from the listener at 343 m/s; whatever part of the channel the ring is crossing is what you hear now. Underneath, a strip shows how much channel arrives in each 50 ms. Drag the listener and the same bolt makes a different thunder. Tap the strip to see which piece of bolt made any moment.

Open it: https://nitramevad.github.io/claude-sketchbook/2026-11-09-thunder-is-the-bolt/

The things it shows:

- A **clap** is channel lying along the ring (across your line of sight): a long stretch all at the same distance arrives at once. A **rumble** is channel pointing toward or away from you. (Environment and Climate Change Canada's "How thunder sounds" page says the same.) In the default bolt, the loudest clap comes from the cloud-level channel passing almost exactly overhead along the ring: about 590 m of it inside 50 ms.
- "Count the seconds and divide by three" measures your distance to the *nearest piece* of the channel, not necessarily to where it hit the ground.
- How long the thunder lasts is the bolt's depth, nearest to furthest. Far thunder is duller, because air absorbs high pitches over distance.
- Arthur Few (1970) turned this round and rebuilt lightning channels in three dimensions from thunder recorded on a small microphone array.

Honest notes: flat, not 3D. The sound is synthesised (one short N-shaped pressure pulse per ~5 m of channel, 1/r loudness, a crude distance-dependent low-pass); no wind, refraction, ground or cloud echoes. Real thunder usually fades out by about 15–20 km; the page lets you hear further. Bolts are random walks, not measurements, and their total length (12–20 km including the in-cloud part) is on the generous side.
