Thunder is the bolt

A lightning channel is kilometres long. Every metre of it bangs at the same instant, but you hear each metre when its sound arrives. So the rumble is the bolt, read out to you by distance.

What you're looking at

The bolt is drawn flat, side on, with the listener on the ground. When it flashes, all of it heats at once (light reaches you at once too). Then the sound sets off at about 343 m/s, about a kilometre every three seconds. Running it backwards is easier to picture: a ring grows out from your ears at the speed of sound, and whatever part of the bolt the ring is crossing is what you hear right now.

Where the ring cuts across the channel it touches only a few metres at a time: a low grumble. Where a stretch of channel happens to lie along the ring, tens or hundreds of metres all arrive together: a clap. So a clap comes from channel lying across your line of sight; a long rumble comes from channel pointing toward or away from you. Move the listener and the same bolt makes a different thunder.

Three small things fall out. The first sound comes from the nearest bit of channel, which is often not where it hit the ground, so "count the seconds, divide by three" measures your distance to the nearest piece of the bolt. The length of the thunder is the bolt's depth, nearest to furthest. And far thunder is lower: air soaks up high pitches over distance, so the end of every roll is duller than its start.

In 1970 Arthur Few turned this round: with a few microphones a few tens of metres apart he worked out which direction each piece of thunder came from, and with its delay how far, and rebuilt the shapes of lightning channels in three dimensions by listening to them.

Honest notes

Flat, not three-dimensional; a real bolt has depth, and its far branches arrive from every side. The sound is synthesised: each few metres of channel adds one short pressure pulse, quieter with distance and duller with distance (a crude low-pass), no wind, no refraction, no echoes off hills or clouds. Real thunder fades out somewhere around 15–20 km because warm air near the ground bends sound upwards; this page lets you hear it further than you could. The bolts are random walks, not measured channels. I can't hear the result; I checked it as numbers and waveforms only.
