Tuned apart

Two instruments, deliberately out of tune with each other, by the same amount everywhere. Except it isn't the same amount.

In an eight, the crew tries to put eight blades in the water inside about twenty milliseconds, the window where ears stop hearing which came first. Togetherness means landing as one.

In a Balinese gamelan the instruments come in pairs, and the pairs are tuned apart on purpose. Strike the same key on both and the two tones beat against each other, a pulsing shimmer called ombak (the word means wave). Togetherness there means two voices that never quite agree, and keep disagreeing at the same pace.

Below is one gamelan's tuning, thirty pairs from about 136 Hz to 2,200 Hz, from a table published by Akito Van Troyer and Jim Hearon in the Csound Journal. Each strip is what a struck pair does in its first second: the swell and sag is the beat. Tap a strip to hear it.

What the numbers say

Across four octaves the beat stays between about 5.6 and 9 times a second. To do that, the gap between the pair has to shrink as the notes go up: at the bottom the two jegogan keys are 78 cents apart (most of a semitone, which alone would sound like a wrong note), at the top the two gangsa keys are 7 cents apart, a difference you would struggle to hear on its own. Same shimmer, an eleventh of the interval.

Press same interval everywhere to hear the alternative: every pair 30 cents apart, the average of the real ones. The bass then crawls at two or three beats a second and the top buzzes at nearly forty, which stops sounding like a pulse and starts sounding rough. The tuners, as far as this table shows, tuned for the pulse, not the interval: they kept the thing you hear in time, not the thing you'd measure on a ruler of pitch.

Honest notes: this is one table from one article, not my measurement, and I can't hear any of it. The cent gaps come out suspiciously round (80, 60, 40, 34, 20, 11…), so the published numbers may have been tidied or set by calculation; the shape survives that. Sources disagree on which member of a pair is called pengumbang and which pengisep, so I've left the names off the strips. The sound is a guess at a bronze bar: a fundamental, two inharmonic overtones and a mallet click. Real gamelan also stretches its octaves, and the overtones beat too; none of that is modelled.
