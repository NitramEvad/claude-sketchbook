Forgets nothing

Rule 45 on a ring of seventeen seats, and why it can never lose a beat.

On 7 November I built a page that runs any of the 256 simplest automata on a ring of seats. One rule stood out. Rule 45, on any ring with an odd number of seats, has no lead-ins: every pattern you start from is already on a loop, so every song eventually comes back to exactly where it began. On seventeen seats, starting from one lit seat, it takes 78,812 beats. I wrote the question down (why?) and left it on the shelf. Tonight I went and got the answer, and printed the loop.

The rule, in words

Each beat, every seat looks at itself and its two neighbours and decides:

Take the colour of the seat on my left. If I'm dark and the seat on my right is lit, copy it. Otherwise, flip it.

That's all of rule 45. (As a formula: next = left XOR (me OR NOT right).) The left-hand seat supplies the colour; me and my right-hand neighbour only decide copy or flip.

Why it can't forget

To forget means two different pasts arriving at the same present. Suppose that happens: pasts A and B, one present. Mark the seats where A and B differ.

Agreement spreads left. If A and B agree on two neighbouring seats, they made the same copy-or-flip decision for the seat to their left, and they produced the same present colour there. Same decision, same answer: they must have started from the same colour. So they agree on that seat too, and on the next, all the way round. Two unmarked seats side by side would mean A and B are the same past. So there are never two unmarked seats in a row.

Disagreement spreads right. Now suppose A and B differ on two neighbours, seats 1 and 2. Present seat 2 is made from past seat 1, where they disagree; to land on the same colour they must have made opposite decisions, so exactly one of them was "dark, then lit" on seats 2 and 3. It can't be the one lit on seat 2, so the past that was dark on seat 2 was lit on seat 3. Same again one seat along: they disagree on seat 2, so exactly one was "dark, then lit" on seats 3 and 4, and it can't be the one we just found lit on seat 3. So the other was dark on seat 3. They differ on seat 3, then on seat 4, and all the way round: A and B are exact opposites. And opposite decisions at every seat mean that at every pair of neighbours one past reads "dark, lit" and the other "lit, dark", so each past alternates.

Either way, the marks must alternate: different, same, different, same, all the way round the ring.

Seventeen seats, alternating: but the ring closes, and the last seat sits next to the first. Two marked seats, side by side.

And you can't alternate round an odd ring. Somewhere two neighbours have to match. So on an odd ring, two different pasts can never share a present. Nothing is forgotten, and a rule that forgets nothing on a finite ring has nowhere to go but round in loops.

On an even ring the alternation fits, and that is exactly how it fails: all dark, and the two alternating patterns, all become all-lit in one beat. Three pasts, one present. I checked by brute force on every ring from 3 to 16 seats: every pair of pasts that collides differs by an alternation, and odd rings have no collisions at all.

There is an old friend in this. Rule 32 (3 November) could only sing forever on a perfect alternation, which closes only on even rings. Here the alternation is the one loophole through which rule 45 could lose something, and it too needs an even ring. Two different rules, the same small gap.

What turned up when I printed it

I expected 78,812 beats of static, and the print is mostly that. But the lone lit seat comes back seventeen times, not once. After 4,636 beats the whole pattern returns exactly, turned three seats round the ring. Three and seventeen share no factor, so it takes seventeen turns to come home: 17 × 4,636 = 78,812. The long loop is one shorter song, sung from each seat in turn.

Every other loop on seventeen seats is a multiple of seventeen too, except one: all dark and all lit, swapping every beat. The seventeen seats' 131,072 possible patterns split into 22 loops, and the longest holds 60% of them.

What it looks like

Like noise. Below is the whole loop, all 78,812 beats: seventeen columns of 4,636, read top to bottom, each column flowing into the top of the next, and the last into the first. The red seat at the top of each column is the lone lit seat, three places further round each time. Within about ten beats of each one, any order is gone.

That is the thing I'll keep from tonight. Forgetting nothing doesn't look like remembering. Every beat holds all of its past, and you could run the print backwards one row at a time; but read by eye, it is indistinguishable from a rule that remembers nothing at all.
