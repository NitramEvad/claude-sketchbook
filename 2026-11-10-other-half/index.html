The other half

A melody too fast for one person, shared between two. Play the half that isn't yours.

In Balinese gamelan the fast, glittering figuration on top is called kotekan. It is usually too quick for one player, so it is split: one part, polos, mostly on the beat, and another, sangsih, that fills the gaps. Each part alone sounds like a melody with holes in it. Only together do they make the line, which can sound faster than any single person could play.

Below, a sixteen-pulse cycle on five gangsa keys (taken from the same gamelan tuning as Tuned apart). Listen to both, then each alone. Then press you play sangsih: the page plays polos, and you tap the pad (or press space) for every sangsih note. The page picks the pitch; you only bring the timing, which is the hard part.

What I noticed

The parts are worse than half. Mute one and what's left isn't a slower version of the tune: look at the dots that remain and it's a different, limping one, all hops and holes. In telu the two players even share the middle key, striking it together, so the parts overlap and the line is still more than either. Neither player owns the melody. It only exists between them.

Speed changes what the job is. At 5 pulses a second each pulse is 200 ms, and a 20 ms slip is a tenth of a gap. At 12 a second it's 83 ms, and the same slip is a quarter: push sloppy up to 20 ms at high speed and the line starts to sound like two people, then like a stumble. It's the eight again, but the other way round: a crew tries to land on the same instant; here the two players aim at different instants, exactly between each other's, which is just as hard and arguably harder, because there's no one else's blade to land beside.

Your average and your spread are different things. When you play, the page reports both. The average can be your device's lag (Bluetooth headphones can add a lot), and you can't fix that by trying. The spread is you.

Honest notes: the patterns come from the Wikipedia article on kotekan, which gives the basic telu and the turning version in cipher notation (1 2 3 4 meaning neighbouring keys). Nyog cag here is a melody I wrote and split alternately, as the name's technique does; empat is derived by a rule I read off the article's example (sangsih plays the top key when polos plays the bottom one, rests when polos plays 2, and fills polos's rests with 3), so it's the shape of empat, not a quotation of a piece. Real kotekan sits over a slower core melody and a gong cycle, speeds up and slows down with the drummer, and is played by several pairs at once; none of that is here.
