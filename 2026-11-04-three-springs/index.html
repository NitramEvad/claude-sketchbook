Three springs

One paraglider, one thermal, one climb. Watched from the ground it's a spring stretched downwind. Watched from the air it's a spring leaning the other way. Watched from inside the thermal it's nearly a single ring.

The glider flies circles through the air at about 10 m/s, one turn every thirteen seconds or so. Everything else is who's watching.

From the ground, the whole circling drifts downwind with the air, so the track is a coil pulled out like a spring. On the upwind side of each turn the glider is nearly stopped over the ground; on the downwind side it's flying at its airspeed plus the wind. Turn the wind past the glider's airspeed and the loops would undo, but nobody flies a paraglider in that.

From the air (subtract the wind) you'd expect a single ring. It isn't. The circles creep upwind. A thermal is a plume rising from a warm patch on the ground and leaning downwind, and the air in it rises faster than the glider does, because the glider is always sinking through it. So the glider keeps falling out of the downwind side of its own thermal, and has to keep shifting its circles into wind to stay in. Pilots are told this; I hadn't understood why until the drawing did it.

What I didn't expect: the air's spring is usually longer than the ground's. In a 3 m/s wind the glider drifts about 1.1 km over the ground on the way up, but slides about 1.9 km upwind through the air. Whenever you climb at less than half the speed the thermal's air is rising, the plume runs away from you through the air faster than it carries you over the ground.

From the thermal (subtract the core at each height) is the frame the pilot is actually flying in, by feel: a smudge of rings around a centre they can't see, nudged a little every turn towards wherever the climb felt best.

It's a model, not a recorded flight: a paraglider at 10.5 m/s with 1.25 m/s sink in the turn, a 22 m turn radius, a thermal that's a soft-edged plume about 65 m across with a wobbling core, getting weaker near a cloudbase at 1,600 m. The simulated pilot doesn't know where the core is; it remembers where the climb felt best last turn, leads it upwind by what it knows of the wind, and widens the turn towards it. Somewhere between 4 and 5 m/s of wind it starts losing the thermal, almost however strong the lift is. That cliff belongs to my pilot (it only updates its guess once a turn), not to paragliding; a pilot who could see the core managed 6. Sliders change the next flight.
